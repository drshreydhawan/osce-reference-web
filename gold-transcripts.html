Orthodontic Patient Referred for Periodontal Assessment

Cluster 1 — History & Risk Factors
Candidate brief 17-year-old boy came to your practice for consultation. He has seen a dentist 12 months ago. You did CPITN scoring today (444/424) and have taken a bitewing x-ray showing bone loss around molars. Intraoral picture showing crowding and deep bite on anterior. Consult with patient regarding findings.
✓ Target: 100/100 — every rubric criterion + both inflection points hit
Patient (Jordan)Hi, um, I was told to come in for a check before I get my braces sorted. Is this gonna take long? I've got footy training after.
CandidateHey Jordan, good to meet you. I'll be as efficient as I can — but I want to flag something up front: your gum measurements and X-rays today showed something I need to look into properly before we can even think about braces. Is it okay if I ask you a few questions first?
PatientOh... okay, I guess. Is something wrong?
CandidateI want to understand a bit more before I explain it properly — that way I can give you the full picture in one go rather than half-answers. First up — how are you going with brushing and flossing at home?
PatientBrush twice a day I reckon. Flossing... not really, to be honest.
CandidateAll good, that's really common — just want the honest picture. Next one, and there's no judgement here at all — do you smoke or vape at all, even just occasionally with mates?
PatientUm... yeah, sometimes. Just vaping, on weekends with friends. Doesn't really count though, right?
CandidateI appreciate you being upfront — and actually it does count, even weekend use affects your gums, so that's really useful for me to know. Two more — has anyone in your family, parents or grandparents, had gum problems or lost teeth relatively young?
PatientActually yeah — Dad lost a couple of back teeth in his thirties, I think from gum stuff.
CandidateThanks, that's important. And on the medical side — does anyone in the family have diabetes or other health conditions?
PatientMy grandma has type 2 diabetes, yeah.
CandidateOkay, Jordan, thank you for being honest about all of that — it genuinely helps. Here's what I found: I measured your gums with a tiny probe today, and healthy gums sit at about 1 to 3 millimetres. In your back teeth, I'm getting 6 millimetres or more everywhere I checked. That, plus the bone loss I can see on your X-ray, tells me something is going on that's well beyond what we'd expect at 17 — it's not a normal part of growing up, and it's not something a simple clean fixes.
PatientWait, that sounds serious. Can't you just clean my teeth and then I can get my braces? I really just want to get this done.
CandidateI hear you, and I get that this isn't what you came in expecting. I want to be straight with you though: if we put braces on while your gums are actively inflamed and losing bone, the forces from the braces can actually speed up that bone loss — in the worst case that puts your teeth at real risk. Sorting the gum issue first actually gives your braces the best chance of working well and lasting.
PatientUgh, okay. So what actually happens now?
CandidateFour things. First, I'm referring you to a periodontist — a gum specialist — because what you have looks like it could be an early-onset, more aggressive type of gum disease, and given your dad's history that fits with a genetic pattern that runs in some families. Second, because your grandmother has type 2 diabetes, I want your GP to run a fasting glucose and HbA1c — there's a real, two-way link between blood sugar and gum disease. Third, I'm going to show you a proper flossing technique today. Fourth — and I say this without judging you at all — even weekend vaping affects gum healing, so cutting it out would genuinely help your treatment work better.
PatientFair enough. And the braces — when can I actually get those?
CandidateOnce the periodontist confirms things are stable — that's the honest answer, I'm not going to give you a date I can't back up. What I can tell you is we'll review you every three months, not once a year, because this needs closer monitoring while it's active. Does that all make sense? Any questions before you head off to training?
PatientNo, that makes sense actually. Thanks for being straight with me, Doc.
Why this hits every mark
  • Names the real agenda before Jordan can assume routine ortho chat (opening line) — Inflection 1 signalled immediately.
  • Asks smoking/vaping directly and non-judgementally — Jordan does not volunteer it unprompted, which is the exact trap the station sets.
  • Elicits BOTH family dental history (dad) AND family medical history (grandmother's diabetes) as separate questions — Inflection 2, the single most differentiating step.
  • Explicitly defers orthodontic treatment with the biological reason stated (forces + inflamed bone = accelerated loss), not just "we'll wait."
  • Both referrals made explicit: periodontist AND GP for fasting glucose/HbA1c.
  • 3-monthly review stated explicitly, not "we'll see."
  • Validates Jordan's frustration ("can't you just clean my teeth") without caving — the exact curveball deflector from the rubric.

Caries Risk Assessment: 8-Year-Old — Early Childhood Caries Pattern

Cluster 1 — History & Risk Factors
Candidate brief 8-year-old child came into your practice with mother. Mother is worried about his teeth. On examination you found a lot of carious teeth. Intraoral picture given for lower jaw, caries on both permanent molars, E/D/C's. Consult with the mother about caries risk assessment and treatment.
✓ Target: 100/100 — pattern recognition + non-judgemental counselling + concrete plan
Patient (Mrs Chen)Hi, thanks for seeing us — I'm really worried, I saw quite a few dark spots when I looked in Liam's mouth last week.
CandidateHi Mrs Chen, hi Liam — lovely to meet you both. Thank you for bringing him in, and there's no judgement here at all, we're just going to work out together what's going on. Before I have a proper look, can I ask a few questions about what Liam eats and drinks day to day?
PatientOf course. He has fruit, some crackers, the odd biscuit — pretty normal kid stuff I think.
CandidateThat's helpful. What about drinks — anything besides water during the day, and does he use a bottle or sippy cup at all, especially at night?
PatientHe still has a bottle of milk before bed, yeah — has done since he was little. Sometimes he falls asleep with it still in his mouth, to be honest.
CandidateThanks for telling me that — really useful. When he does fall asleep with it, do you usually get a chance to brush his teeth afterwards?
PatientNot always, no. If he's already asleep I don't want to wake him up.
CandidateThat makes complete sense as a parent. Now I'm going to have a look at Liam's teeth — Liam, I'm just going to count your teeth, like a little game, okay?
PatientOkay, um... how bad is it, Doctor?
CandidateMrs Chen, there's quite a bit of decay, particularly on his upper front teeth, and also on some of his new adult molars at the back. What's really telling is the pattern — the upper front teeth are affected but the lower front teeth are relatively okay. That's actually a really recognisable sign of what we call prolonged bottle caries.
PatientWait, but I only gave him milk — I thought that was healthy! Are you saying I did something wrong?
CandidateYou're absolutely right that milk is healthy — full of calcium, great for growing teeth and bones. The issue isn't the milk itself, it's purely the timing. When Liam falls asleep with the bottle, the milk pools around his upper front teeth for hours. During sleep saliva flow almost stops completely, so there's nothing washing that milk sugar away, and the lower teeth are actually shielded by his tongue and lip, which is why they look better. This is one of the most common patterns we see — you have done nothing wrong, and the fact you've brought him in means we can fix this together.
PatientOkay, that's a relief to hear. So what do we do about it?
CandidateTwo things run in parallel. First, the habit itself — I'd suggest weaning off the bottle over the next two to four weeks, diluting the milk a little more each night until it's just water. Once he's asleep, water only from now, and the single biggest thing you can do straight away is brush his teeth right before he goes down, using a pea-sized amount of fluoride toothpaste. Second, we do need to treat the decay itself — I'll prioritise his new adult molars and anything causing pain, and I'll go through each tooth once we've got bitewing X-rays back.
PatientWill he need to be put to sleep for any of it? I've heard scary stories.
CandidateGood question — most of what I'm expecting here we can manage with local anaesthetic in the chair, with a bit of patience and some breaks. If it turns out more complex once we see the X-rays, I'd refer to a paediatric dentist rather than push through something Liam isn't coping with — but let's see what today's X-rays show first.
PatientOkay, that sounds reasonable. When do you want to see us again?
CandidateFour weeks — that gives the weaning plan time to take hold and we can start treatment on the priority teeth then. I'll write down the diet and brushing plan for you to take home so you're not trying to remember it all. You're clearly a really engaged mum, Liam is lucky to have you looking out for him.
Why this hits every mark
  • Structured feeding history specifically probes bottle use, timing relative to sleep, and post-bottle brushing — not a generic "what does he eat" question.
  • Explicitly links the upper-anterior/lower-anterior-sparing pattern to the nighttime bottle — the key diagnostic pivot, stated out loud rather than assumed.
  • Explains the cariogenic mechanism in plain language (saliva flow drop, lactose, tongue/lip shielding) without jargon.
  • Handles the "I only gave him milk, did I do something wrong?" curveball with immediate validation, not defensiveness or over-reassurance that skips the actual explanation.
  • Gives a concrete, staged weaning plan (dilution over 2–4 weeks) plus one immediate harm-reduction step (brush before bed) rather than a vague "cut back."
  • Names paediatric dentist referral as a contingency, not a default — shows judgement rather than reflexive escalation.

Management of Denture Stomatitis in a Diabetic Patient

Cluster 2 — Balanced (History informs management)
Candidate brief 65-year-old lady, wearing an upper denture for 38 years. Current denture is 20 years old. Complains of sore mouth and poor-fitting denture. On examination: red inflamed palate, buccal flange overextension, all teeth worn out. Consult with the patient about management.
✓ Target: 100/100 — full multifactorial diagnosis, correct sequencing, no premature Rx
Patient (Mrs Walsh)Doctor, this old denture of mine is really sore lately and it keeps popping out when I yawn or eat. I was hoping you could just make me a new one.
CandidateThanks for coming in, Mrs Walsh — before we talk about a new denture, I want to understand exactly what's been going on with this one, and with your mouth underneath it, so we get this right. How long have you had this particular denture?
PatientAbout twenty years now, I suppose I should've come in sooner.
CandidateNo judgement at all — we'll sort it out. Do you take it out at night, or do you tend to sleep in it?
PatientI sleep in it, always have. Never had a problem before.
CandidateOkay, and how do you clean it at the moment — do you soak it in anything, or use a cleaning tablet?
PatientJust a rinse under the tap most days, honestly.
CandidateThanks for being upfront. I also want to ask about your general health — you mentioned diabetes on your form. Do you know roughly what your last blood sugar or HbA1c reading was?
PatientNot off the top of my head, no — I haven't seen my GP in a little while either.
CandidateThat's alright, we'll get that sorted through a referral. Has your mouth felt dry at all lately?
PatientNow that you mention it, yes, quite dry actually. I've been sucking on boiled lollies to help with that.
CandidateThat's really useful to know, thank you. Right, let me tell you what I'm seeing and what I think is going on. The roof of your mouth is quite red and inflamed — this is a condition called denture stomatitis, and it's caused by a fungal infection, thrush essentially, growing on the fitting surface of the denture. In your case a few things have come together: sleeping in the denture means the fungus never gets a break, the light cleaning means it isn't being killed off regularly, the dry mouth reduces your natural protection, and your blood sugar being a bit higher than ideal makes it harder for your body to fight the infection.
PatientI've worn this to bed for twenty years and never had a problem — why do I need to stop now? And can't you just give me something to clear it up and make the new denture?
CandidateI completely understand it feels normal after all these years. The difference now is your blood sugar running a bit high, which means your body's defences aren't quite what they were — the denture is acting like a home for the fungus, and if it stays in overnight, the fungus just keeps growing and the palate never gets a chance to recover. On the new denture — if we made one now, before this clears up, the new one would get infected too within weeks. So the sequence really matters here.
PatientAlright, fair enough. So what do I actually need to do?
CandidateFrom tonight, out at bedtime, every night — no exceptions. When it's out, rinse it, brush it gently with a soft brush (not toothpaste, that scratches it), then soak it overnight in a cleaning tablet like Polident or Steradent. I'm also going to prescribe an antifungal gel, Daktarin — dab a small amount on the fitting surface of the denture and directly on the roof of your mouth, four times a day for four weeks. And on the lollies — I know they help with the dryness, but they're feeding the fungus with sugar; there are sugar-free saliva sprays that do the same job for dryness without that problem.
PatientAnd the new denture — when can we actually start that?
CandidateOnce the infection's cleared and your blood sugar is better controlled — I'll write to your GP today about that HbA1c review, ideally getting it under 7%. I'd like to see you back in two to four weeks to check the palate's settled, and we'll plan the new denture from there, this time getting the fit right so it doesn't overextend on the cheek like this one does. Does that plan make sense?
PatientYes, that's clearer now, thank you for explaining it properly.
Why this hits every mark
  • Elicits overnight wear, hygiene routine, AND HbA1c/diabetes control as three separate, deliberate questions before diagnosing.
  • Explicitly identifies the boiled-lolly habit as a xerostomia coping strategy AND a candidal sugar source — a two-layer finding many candidates miss.
  • Names Newton's Type II denture stomatitis and explains the full multifactorial aetiology (hygiene, overnight wear, xerostomia, hyperglycaemia) without jargon overload.
  • Handles "I've worn it to bed 20 years, why stop now?" without dismissing her long habit, while still holding the clinical line.
  • Explicitly refuses to build a new denture before infection resolution + glycaemic optimisation — the single highest-yield sequencing point in this station.
  • Gives correct miconazole application (denture fitting surface AND palate, QID, 4 weeks) rather than just "use this cream."

Extraction Planning for a Patient on Denosumab and Warfarin

Cluster 2 — Balanced (History informs management)
Candidate brief Mr John Smith, 67, attends with swelling and mobility of the lower right second molar (grade 3, abscess). History of MI, hypertension (atenolol, warfarin), hypothyroidism (thyroxine), osteoporosis (calcium, denosumab — last injection 2 days ago). Also on Panadeine Forte. Manage his concern regarding the mobile tooth 47.
✓ Target: 100/100 — full medication history, correct MRONJ + warfarin reasoning, no cessation errors
Patient (Mr Smith)Doc, this back tooth is killing me — it's swollen and it's wobbling. Can you just take it out today?
CandidateMr Smith, I can see you're uncomfortable — before I explain the tooth, I need to go through everything you're taking, because a couple of your medications directly change how safely we can treat you today. Can you talk me through all your current medications?
PatientSure — I take warfarin and a tablet called atenolol for my blood pressure, since I had a heart attack a few years back. Thyroxine for my thyroid. Calcium tablets, and an injection for my bones — had that just two days ago actually. And I take Panadeine Forte for the pain right now.
CandidateThank you — that injection is really important, can you confirm the name and how often you get it?
PatientDenosumab, I think — every six months, been on it about three years now.
CandidateAnd your warfarin — do you know your most recent INR?
PatientNot off hand, no. It's usually stable though.
CandidateOkay. Now let me tell you what I've found — that lower right back molar has a significant infection and is grade 3 mobile, meaning it moves quite a lot in all directions. Unfortunately it can't be saved and does need to come out.
PatientRight, so let's get it out then. My old dentist always told me to stop my warfarin before anything like this — should I stop it now?
CandidateThat advice was appropriate for a lot of dental work in the past, but current guidelines are clear: for a straightforward extraction, we don't stop warfarin. Stopping it actually puts you at greater risk — you've had a heart attack, so a clot forming is far more dangerous than a bit of extra bleeding we can manage locally with stitches, a special dressing, and a mouthwash called tranexamic acid. I will get your INR checked in the next day or two to confirm it's in a safe range first.
PatientFair enough. And the bone injection — is that a problem?
CandidateIt is the more pressing issue, actually. Denosumab slows down the cells that break down bone, which is great for your osteoporosis, but it can occasionally make it harder for the jawbone to heal properly after an extraction — a condition called MRONJ. Because your last injection was only two days ago, you're in the highest-risk window for that, so I don't want to rush into pulling the tooth out today.
PatientSo what, you're just going to leave an infected tooth in my mouth?
CandidateNot at all — I'll treat the infection today. I'll prescribe antibiotics, and if there's a pocket of pus I can drain it now to give you relief. What I want to do before the extraction itself is speak with your GP or the specialist managing your osteoporosis, to plan the safest timing relative to your denosumab cycle. That might mean days rather than weeks — I'm not putting this off indefinitely, I just don't want to extract into the highest-risk window without a plan.
PatientOkay, that makes me feel a bit better actually. What antibiotic, and will it interact with anything?
CandidateAmoxicillin, three times a day for five days — it's compatible with your other medications, though I will let you know that some antibiotics can affect warfarin, so I'll keep an eye on that and may ask your GP to recheck your INR while you're on it. For pain, keep taking the Panadeine Forte as directed, and avoid anti-inflammatories like ibuprofen for now given the warfarin.
PatientUnderstood. So what's the actual plan from here?
CandidateToday: antibiotics, drainage if there's a collection, pain relief. This week: I contact your GP and your osteoporosis specialist to agree on timing and confirm your INR. Once we've got the green light, we extract using a gentle technique, stitch the socket closed, and see you at one week and again at four weeks to make sure it's healing properly. Any questions about any of that before you go?
PatientNo, that's a really clear plan actually. Thanks, Doc.
Why this hits every mark
  • Elicits denosumab dose/frequency/duration AND the critical 2-day-since-last-injection detail before touching the extraction decision.
  • Correctly refuses to stop warfarin, gives the actual reason (thromboembolic risk in an MI patient outweighs bleeding risk), and names concrete local haemostatic measures.
  • Treats the 2-day denosumab window as the reason to temporise (drain + antibiotics today, specialist liaison before definitive extraction) rather than either refusing treatment entirely or extracting recklessly.
  • Handles "so you're just leaving an infected tooth in?" without sounding evasive — gives a concrete same-day action (antibiotics ± drainage) alongside the deferral.
  • Flags the warfarin–antibiotic interaction awareness point, not just "here's your antibiotic."
  • Closes with an explicit staged plan (today / this week / once cleared) rather than a vague "we'll be in touch."

Cluster 3 — Evaluation & Treatment Planning
Candidate brief Patient (50+) attended Friday afternoon, lives two hours' drive away, with pain and swelling on an upper molar. On examination the tooth was badly decayed; you attempted extraction and the crown broke. X-ray shows the roots in close proximity to the maxillary sinus. Patient takes controlled blood-pressure medication. Inform the patient about the broken crown and discuss the next step.
✓ Target: 100/100 — immediate honest disclosure, safe plan, size-dependent OAC reasoning, travel safety-net
CandidateMr Doyle, I need to stop for a moment and tell you honestly what's just happened — the crown, the visible part of the tooth, has broken away during the extraction. I want to place some gauze here for you. You're still fully numb, so you shouldn't be feeling any pain. Are you okay?
Patient (Mr Doyle)Broke off? Hang on — did something go wrong? Are you sure you know what you're doing?
CandidateThat's a completely fair question, and I want to be straight with you. The crown breaking during an extraction is a recognised complication, especially when a tooth has been as heavily decayed as this one — it doesn't mean anything was done incorrectly. But it does mean the roots are still in your jaw and we need to change our approach to remove them safely. I'm not going to rush or guess.
PatientRight. So can't you just grab the roots with the pliers and pull them out? I just want it over with — I've got a two-hour drive home.
CandidateI understand you want this finished today, and so do I. CandidateTwo options. I can do a small surgical procedure here — make a tiny cut in the gum, section the roots, and remove them piece by piece under direct vision. CandidateIt depends on the size. A very small one — up to about 3 millimetres — we can usually manage with a single stitch to stabilise the clot plus some sinus precautions. So the plan scales to what we actually find — I'm not going to leave you with a problem unaddressed.

CandidateTake paracetamol and ibuprofen — I'd alternate them, so something roughly every three to four hours, and take the first dose before the anaesthetic wears off so you stay ahead of it. Is there someone who can drive you if you're not feeling up to it?
PatientMy wife's in the waiting room, she can drive. Okay — I think I'd rather you sort it today if it's safe, honestly.
CandidateThat's reasonable, and I think a surgical removal here is within what I can do safely. I'll get written consent first, take my time, and if at any point it looks more complex than expected, I'll stop and arrange the oral surgeon rather than push on. I'll document everything and give your wife the after-hours details too. Any questions before we go ahead?
