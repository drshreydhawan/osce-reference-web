Early Childhood Caries — Mehmat Khan, 8PQ-2

Module 1, Cluster 1. Also called "Mustafa" in some variants. Father/mother worried about teeth turning black — decayed primary teeth and decayed permanent molars.

Patient & Scenario

Stays with grandparents/daycare on weekdays. Access barrier: family recently moved from a different country. Everyone (all caregivers) should be involved in the diet chart. Consent needed for investigation.

  • Variation: single father ↔ single mother (Mustafa Khan) — sometimes anti-fluoride.
  • Variation: early childhood caries via milk bottle.
  • Variation: antihistamine ↔ cough syrup.

How to Start / Questions to Ask

"Hello! My name is Dr Akhai, I'll be your dentist today. Nice to meet you Mr Khan and hello Mehmat. You are concerned about his teeth turning black — what worries you? Did Mehmat complain about anything? When did you first notice this?"

  1. You are concerned about teeth turning black — what worries you, dad? Will this affect adult teeth?
  2. When did you last visit a dentist? Any specific reason?
  3. Why hasn't he been to the dentist before? (possible answer: can't afford it — mention Child Dental Benefit Schedule, time constraints)
  4. Did he complain about anything? How does he clean his teeth? Supervision? In-between teeth?
  5. How is his diet? (cookies/cocoa cereal — note: "my other children had the same diet") What does he like to drink? Water? What toothpaste?
  6. Any illnesses in childhood? (few illnesses in a short time + high-frequency snacking = high risk)

Diagnosis & Risk Factors

Provisional diagnosis: dental caries — high risk of dental caries. "Would you like me to walk you through why he has this problem?"

Risk factors: Diet (F.A.S.T. mnemonic — Frequency, Amount, Sticky, Time); bacteria (poor hygiene); acids reducing minerals from teeth (linked to decreased water intake/dehydration, and medicine such as cough syrup/antihistamine); low motivation (no remineralisation, less saliva).

High risk = more than 3 lesions. 8-year-olds are in transition to becoming independent with hygiene. Less water → caries / constipation / dry mouth.

Investigation & Management

Investigation: extra-oral — jaws, jaw joint, how he's closing his mouth (lip incompetence, clicking); intra-oral — count and check decay, palpate, mobility, percuss, blow air on teeth to find sensitive/painful ones; X-ray — 2 bitewings (right/left), OPG if removing any teeth; diet chart (Mehmat/daycare/grandparents), 2 weekdays + 1 weekend day.

Management — TG Caries framework: diet modification, plaque reduction, tooth surface modification, saliva modification.

  1. Preventive — tap water, diet modification, fluoride varnish (note CPP-ACP allergy caution), pit and fissure sealant, adult toothpaste.
  2. Small decay — GIC/composite (painful tooth → reversible pulpitis → GIC).
  3. Deep decay — pulpotomy/pulpectomy.
  4. Extraction — space maintainer.

Motivating independent brushing: let him choose a colourful brush, floss with a holder, plaque-glo toothpaste. "I understand it's challenging to manage by yourself. We need to work as a team — I'll demonstrate brushing and flossing. If the problem is not addressed now he could require extensive treatment involving cost and time. Maybe we can give him an electric brush."

Questions Patients Ask & Key Facts
  • "Don't know what he's eating at school?" / "I don't think that's a permanent molar?" / "I'm not rich and can't afford it?" — check eligibility (household income under ~$95,400 single, ~$152,640 couple) and Child Dental Benefit Scheme ($1,095 over 2 calendar years).
  • "Did it affect his sleep or eating?"
  • Prefer to keep baby teeth as long as possible — they act as a natural space maintainer.
  • Not drinking enough water can lead to kidney stones/constipation; tap water has the added benefit of fluoride.
  • References: caries protective & risk factors; TG Caries — Dry Mouth (Evans); caries risk assessment; Child Benefit Scheme.

Ortho Referral with Undiagnosed Periodontitis — Jordan, 17PQ-1

Module 1, Cluster 1. Crowded teeth; wants braces; last dentist visit 12 months ago; CPITN scores taken today.

Patient & Scenario

CPITN (Community Periodontal Index of Treatment Needs) rows: 4, 4, 4 / 4, 2, 4 — a score of 4 means complete periodontal charting is required. X-ray shows bone loss around molars. No smoking.

Not mentioned unless asked: bullied at school because of crooked teeth; never been to a dentist before; grandmother has diabetes; family history of early tooth loss (parents wearing plates/dentures); "no one wanted to go out on a date with him."

How to Start / Questions to Ask

"Hi Jordan, thanks for coming in today. My name is Dr Akhai. I understand you are concerned about crowded teeth and want to improve the alignment of your teeth. We will definitely discuss options about that, but before that, is it okay if I ask a few questions?"

  1. Tell me more about your issue — any wobbly teeth? Any bleeding?
  2. "With ortho treatment, cleaning can get a bit difficult, so how are you maintaining your teeth?" — then explain your CPITN findings and diagnosis.
  3. Do you have anyone in the family with diabetes? Have you noticed anyone wearing plates/missing teeth? Does anyone have crooked teeth (tooth/jaw size discrepancy)?
  4. "Do you think it will be a good idea to explore why you are having this problem?" — their family history.
  5. How's your general health? Blood tests? Did your dentist mention anything about your gums?

Diagnosis & Risk Factors

"Treatment to improve your alignment will temporarily make cleaning difficult. Earlier, while checking your gums, I was calling out some numbers... I have given a score of 4 in these spaces which means the space between your gums and teeth is 5.5mm, which is very unusual for your age. Also you can see black spaces on your x-ray — from what it seems like you have a condition called periodontitis (irritation of gums and bone). If we use these teeth as anchors for braces you might lose these teeth."

Risk factors: oral hygiene/crowding; family history (hyper-responsiveness/aggressive bacteria); underlying medical condition (lowered immunity, stress).

Differential: depends on history — rapidly progressing periodontitis, or periodontitis as a manifestation of systemic disease (diabetes / cyclic neutropenia / leukaemia). Type 1 diabetes tends to cause more destruction around first molars, as these are the first teeth to erupt.

Investigation & Management

Investigation: extra-oral — face, jaws, muscles, mouth closing; intra-oral — teeth, gums, deposits, cold test, complete 6-point charting (probing, mobility, recession, bleeding on probing, furcations); X-ray — OPG (or bitewings if given), perio pictures, periapical/vertical bitewings if OPG given.

Management: refer to periodontist to treat the gum condition; GP for blood test. Today — assess effectiveness of biofilm reduction. Pre-requisites before referring to a periodontist: manage general dental care, detailed periodontal charting, current OPG.

"What will the specialist do?" — Gum specialist will (1) raise the gums and clean under direct vision, (2) may give antibiotics as part of treatment, (3) discuss the option of bone/artificial material to allow bone to grow (downsides: surgery, cost, time, can fail).

Questions Patients Ask & Key Facts
  • "I have saved and I'm keen to get braces started, can you give me a referral to an orthodontist?" — "I am happy to refer you, but my recommendation is..."
  • "Do I need a blood test? What will you see?" / "What does 4 (score) mean?" → probing depth >5.5mm.
  • "Which specialist will you refer me to?" / "How long do I have this condition for?" / "What is the underlying medical condition responsible for this?"
  • Periodontitis with a genetic predisposition has pubertal onset. Review 3-monthly until gum condition/immune system is stable.
  • References: TG — Periodontitis; DHSV referral criteria; Odell — Crowding case; ADA in-patient perio.

Denture Stomatitis & Angular Cheilitis — Mrs/Mr Smart (Campbell), 65PQ-3

Module 1, Cluster 2. Current denture 20 years old; few natural teeth; doesn't wear the lower denture; ill-fitting, falls out.

Patient & Scenario

Presenting reason: angular cheilitis. Not mentioned unless asked: diabetes — a major assessment area, often not volunteered. Well-managed diabetes can be disrupted by a broken denture, since the patient can't chew properly.

  • Variation: angular/aphthous cheilitis — symptomatic (no photo) vs. asymptomatic (photo given).
  • Variation: denture stomatitis alone.
  • Variation: wants a new denture ↔ wants the same one repaired.
  • Variation: wears the denture at night ↔ leaves it out. Rarely: combination syndrome.
  • Note: an old, broken, ill-fitting denture can still cause denture stomatitis on its own.

How to Start / Questions to Ask

"Mrs Smart, I understand you are here today because you are experiencing some issues with your denture. Before we discuss how to manage this issue, I would like to clearly understand the problems you are facing with this denture and understand your expectations — could you tell me more about your problem?"

  1. Why do you want a new or repaired denture? How is this denture affecting your day-to-day life?
  2. How did the denture break? (→ denture hygiene) Apart from the broken denture, is there any other concern?
  3. How is your general health? How are you managing your diabetes? When was your last blood test?
  4. How is the broken/loose denture affecting your diet? Can you chew food?
  5. "You haven't been regular with your dentist and GP — is there a specific reason?" "Why do you wear the denture at night?" (partner doesn't know about it / doesn't like the sensation without it)

Diagnosis & Risk Factors

"Thanks for sharing that information. Normally I would expect the body's immune system to fight back, but in your case that didn't happen." Candida is a disease of the immune system.

Denture stomatitis: poor hygiene/ill-fitting denture, diabetes, dietary factors, smoking +/-. Angular cheilitis: skin folds (denture over-extension), dietary deficiency, GI issues, skin conditions.

Denture stomatitis is noticed by the dentist, not the patient — don't shift the initial focus. Leaving it untreated can lead to hyperplastic candidiasis. Denture should ideally be changed every 8–10 years.

Investigation & Management

Option 1: numbing gel, swab test, blood test. Option 2: antifungal cream (won't work if due to dietary deficiency).

Management: reline — cheap/less effective (chairside) vs. lab (stronger, checks bite, extra cost); do nothing (disadvantage: papillary hyperplasia) vs. denture hygiene (leave out at night, clean with soap and water). GP blood test for diabetes/nutritional deficiency; oral medicine referral.

Review pathway: 2 days → 2 weeks → 1 month; no improvement → specialist advice (systemic antifungal); improvement → new denture (recommend upper + lower partial).

Prescription: Miconazole 2% gel, 2.5ml, 4 times daily; apply to denture surface twice a day.

Questions Patients Ask & Key Facts
  • "I don't like using soap and water to clean my denture?" → alternative is Polident tablets.
  • "Is it a fungal or bacterial infection?" → ~90% fungal. "Is Vaseline good for sore corners?" → yes, keeps the area moist.
  • "Why do I need a lower denture?" → to shift the biting forces. "Can we do the denture now?" → no, fit might be affected by current inflammation.
  • Denture stomatitis: sensitive erythematous lesions, associated with Candida, confined to denture-bearing areas (particularly the palate). Angular cheilitis: painful erythema/fissuring of the mouth corners, most commonly Candida (also S. aureus, streptococci) — often self-resolves once predisposing factors are addressed.
  • References: TG — Denture stomatitis / Angular cheilitis.

MRONJ / Odontogenic Infection — David Arthur, 67PQ-4PQ-5

Module 1, Cluster 2. Abscess, mobile tooth (grade 3), already prescribed Panadeine Forte for pain.

Patient & Scenario

History: myocardial infarction/hypertension (sometimes); on Atenolol + Verapamil + Warfarin; hypothyroidism → thyroxine; osteoporosis → calcium and denosumab; sometimes allergic to penicillin.

  • Variation: tooth mobile ↔ not mobile.
  • Variation: acute periodontal abscess ↔ acute periapical abscess (RCT/non-vital).
  • High chance MRONJ is already present — check for lump/swelling around the neck (lymphadenitis).

How to Start / Questions to Ask

"Mr Arthur, thank you for being patient while I was examining your mouth. I understand the tooth on the bottom right has been troubling you; in order to understand better, can I ask a few questions?"

  1. Tell me more about the swelling/pain? Warfarin — when was your last INR? Social history? Last dental visit — what was done?
  2. Osteoporosis — diagnosis? Medication? How long?
  3. Do you know how this medication works? Are you aware of the side effects? Do you smoke? Do your gums bleed? (sign of active gum disease, which can cause MRONJ)
  4. Don't combine antibiotics with active dental treatment.

Diagnosis & Risk Factors

"Mr Arthur, this problem started a few days ago and you have a gum boil around that tooth called a Dental Abscess — we confirmed this infection is coming from the gum by applying some cold on your tooth (which gave a response), and on the x-ray there's a black area — bone loss." (Diagnosis: acute periodontal abscess / dental abscess.)

MRONJ (definition, TG): exposed area of bone not healing for more than 8 weeks in a patient taking antiresorptive drugs — a complication of the drug, not just the treatment. MRONJ can be missed as symptoms mimic a periodontal abscess. For spreading infection, always check for facial swelling.

Key facts: Atenolol + Verapamil usually not given together (drug interaction — can suddenly raise blood pressure). Thyroxine and calcium taken together impede thyroxine absorption. Any active infection not treated within 24 hours needs antibiotics.

Investigation & Management

"We have a few options to manage this — do you have anything particular on your mind? Removing the tooth will get rid of the infection, but I am concerned about healing."

  1. Do nothing.
  2. Denosumab pathway — depends on age/mandible, perio infection, ENT specialist advice.
  3. Warfarin pathway — check INR (<3.5 acceptable); thyroxine interactions; metronidazole increases bleeding risk. If INR ok: drain and splint.
  4. Antibiotics + splint pathway — remove deposit above the gum line (extra cost); pain killer, avoid NSAIDs.
  5. Temporise or refer to hospital specialist/GP.

If on Warfarin, can't go below the gum line without checking INR first. "If you want me to remove the tooth, I'll take advice from the specialist (my recommendation is doing it with him)."

Questions Patients Ask & Key Facts
  • "What will the specialist do?" / "Can MRONJ happen with the specialist too?" / "If the medication can lead to MRONJ, can I stop it?"
  • "Do you see anything on the radiograph?" / "Why can't you remove the tooth today?" → refer to Oral & Maxillofacial Surgery.
  • Ensuring optimal oral hygiene with regular review reduces MRONJ risk — power brush, floss for interdental cleaning.
  • References: TG — MRONJ/odontogenic infection; Odell case; BDJ "Preexisting MRONJ"; American Association of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgeons — MRONJ.

Crown Broke During Extraction — Esposito / Shell Parker, 50PQ-6

Module 1, Cluster 3. Tooth 16/26, badly decayed, breaks at the gumline mid-extraction; swelling on the face near the ear; lives 2 hours away, Friday afternoon; sometimes penicillin allergy.

How to Start / Breaking the News

"Mr/Mrs Esposito, I'd like to update you on what has happened during the procedure, but first — are you comfortable the way you are seated?" Start by acknowledging and apologising. "The tooth that I was removing was badly decayed and had a weak structure. While I was removing the tooth carefully with my forceps, the top part of the tooth has broken and the roots are still inside. I am sorry about that."

  1. "I am sorry this has happened, not something we were hoping for — wanted you to have a peaceful weekend. There are a few options we can discuss, but before that would it be OK if I ask a few questions?"
  2. What are you worried about? Now that this has happened, what do you expect me to do? What do you think might happen?
  3. Can I take one more X-ray to show you but also as part of my record? (needs a 2nd consent)
  4. Would you like me to speak about replacement options?

Management

"It's very unfortunate the top portion of the tooth has come out. It's still within my scope of practice to remove it. A different technique, which I am happy to discuss with you — there are other options as well, as you have swelling on the face. Booking you with the hospital or a specialist next week — which one?"

  • Do nothing (adv/disadv).
  • Extraction by me → burs/handpiece to divide the roots, create space for a fine instrument, possibly a small cut in the gum → stitches (resorbable). Complications: (1) OAC, (2) root slip → refer specialist (Caldwell-Luc approach); leave it / review 48–72hr.
  • Temporise & refer to hospital: pulp extirpation/antibiotics, specialist referral, call after 24hrs to check how they are doing.

New topic — OAC (oro-antral communication) guidance: <2mm → suture, sinus precautions (no nose blowing, no straw, no forceful sneeze/cough, open-mouth sneeze), follow up 2 weeks. ≥2–3mm → haemostatic agent (collagen/cellulose) + figure-of-8 suture. >3mm → buccal advancement flap, done by a surgeon/urgent referral.

Questions Patients Ask & Key Facts
  • Which antibiotic? Can you leave the root piece in the sinus? (if small/not infected, no sinusitis — no issue) What if something bad happens to me? Will you cover the cost of a specialist? (No.)
  • Do you think these complications can happen with the specialist too? (Yes, but they're better equipped to manage them.)
  • Breaking-bad-news structure: give the information in 20–30 seconds, then pause and let them process and speak.
  • References: DHSV referral criteria; spreading odontogenic infection (TG); ADA news — difficult conversations.

Replacement Options, Elderly Patient — Mr Ken, 78PQ-7

Module 1, Cluster 3. Scaling done, no new cavities, gums healthy; tooth #6 has a metal cap; wants to know replacement options; hypertension not initially volunteered.

Patient & Scenario

Complaint about quality of a previous RCT is possible — offer 2 advantages/2 disadvantages of each replacement option; patient might say "I'm not interested in replacement." Variations: normal gap ↔ small gap; age 48/60/78; grinding can affect replacement options; dry mouth/hypertension might affect choice — respect autonomy, don't force options.

How to Start / Questions to Ask

"Mr Ken, I am done with examination and cleaning — everything looks great, you are doing really well with your hygiene. The space of that missing tooth I have been monitoring closely still looks the same. Do you have any concerns? Is it OK if I update your medical history?"

  1. Has anything changed? Which medication are you taking? (if unsure: "is it OK if I reach out to your GP to confirm which medicine you are taking?")
  2. Are you aware how these medicines affect your oral condition? (hypertension medicines → dry mouth) Any dry mouth lately?
  3. How did you lose that tooth?

Key Facts & Management

Drug-induced dry mouth reduces saliva/buffering and increases caries and periodontal disease risk. Apart from calcium channel blockers, all BP meds cause dry mouth. Flexible denture (thermoplastic nylon resin) can be given to patients allergic to acrylic resin. Ante's Law: balance is lost if you lose any of these teeth to decay or gum disease. "This space right now is stable — as long as other teeth are OK, we can maintain this space."

Replacement options tree: do nothing (adv/disadv); removable denture — acrylic, metal, or flexible (preferred among removable, though it can aggravate existing perio disease); bridge — 2-unit cantilever or 3-unit; implant (expensive) — short/mini/normal, CBCT.

  • Metal partial denture — thin/comfortable/durable, has a clasp/expensive; can't add teeth, clasp can put extra force on an already weak tooth, difficult to clean.
  • Acrylic partial denture — lower cost, can add teeth; bulkier, less durable.
  • Flexible denture (preferred) — clasp-free, flexible, lower cost; can't add teeth, not as durable.
  • Cantilever (preferred, 2-unit) — ideally uses the tooth behind for support; risk of debonding increased if that tooth is compromised.
  • 3-unit bridge — not ideal if the abutment tooth is compromised, but good appearance/predictability/easy hygiene.
  • Implant (preferred at 48) — fixed, doesn't involve other teeth; downsides are cost, nerve risk, specialist referral, CBCT.

Small-gap variation: do nothing is not a good option here — drifting teeth lead to food impaction/gum disease/decay, and a tilted tooth might need trimming or ortho uprighting. Mini implant preferred; cantilever if the patient doesn't want an implant.

Questions Patients Ask & Key Facts
  • Which option is best for me? I have enough money. Can you put a clasp on that tooth? Am I too old for ortho?
  • Did my old dentist do a bad job? Is there anything wrong with my filling? What are the disadvantages of a cantilever?
  • Can't use a short-filled tooth as an anchor for a bridge — take specialist opinion. A cap on a tooth affects the clasp of a removable partial denture.
  • Long-term dentures can be damaging as forces transmit directly to the gums. "If you have any concerns about the care you have received, I suggest you reach out to your dentist."
  • References: Dry Mouth (TG); indications/contraindications of cantilever; Journal of Prosthetic Dentistry (short implant).

Congenital Syphilis — Stevie, 8PQ-8

Module 2, Cluster 1. Here today with foster carer Mrs Kaur; carer concerned about appearance — shape of teeth, puffy gums.

Patient & Scenario

Partial eyesight/hearing loss; high caries risk — referral needed, preventive advice; difficulty holding things; open bite. Not given: mouth breathing; bullied at school; upcoming family wedding.

Variation: short-term carer (consent for investigation only) ↔ long-term carer (consent for treatment, specialist consent required).

How to Start / Questions to Ask

"Hello Mrs Kaur, thank you for bringing Stevie today. I understand you have some concerns about her teeth. What are you worried about?"

  1. What does Stevie think about it? Did she complain about anything?
  2. "Because her teeth are a bit unusual compared to others, is there something in her medical history you are aware of?"
  3. Why do you want to fix her teeth? Apart from shape, is there any other concern? Does she have any medical problem?
  4. "I understand you are keen to get her treated, but I would like to know what kind of consent you have." If she has difficulty holding things, how is she brushing?

Diagnosis & Risk Factors

Signs & symptoms: puffy gums (could be plaque-related); shape of teeth — developmental: Hutchinson's incisors (notched), mulberry molars (bumpy), peg lateral, cyst (fluid-filled); gaps — widely spaced, extra teeth, delayed eruption, frenum.

Risk factors: hypoplastic teeth, open bite (mouth breathing), pits on molars, reduced dexterity.

Syphilis — T. Pallidum → eighth nerve deafness, Hutchinson's triad. Can't give the diagnosis of syphilis if the carer hasn't been informed of her medical condition — use the term "developmental defect." Open bite → mouth breathing → dry mouth.

Investigation & Management

Investigation: extra-oral — face, jaw, mouth opening/lip closure; intra-oral — visual (count teeth, gums, plaque, decay, colour changes), tactile (palpate, foramen blanches on pressure), percuss, mobility, blow air; X-ray — BW/OPG → BPE; pictures for records; diet chart. Confirm dental history first ("Could you tell me how she takes care of her teeth?"). Ask the organisation to confirm if they hold any medical records/diagnosis for her. Separate consent needed to assess general health.

BPE age ranges: 7–11 yrs → codes 0–2 (teeth 16, 11, 26, 36, 31, 46); age 12 onwards → codes 0–4.

Management: "When you come back with consent, can fix the shape of her teeth for the wedding." Other concerns (spaces/jaw defects) need complex treatment and specialist referral — separate consent required (pedo/ortho).

Health promotion: show/demonstrate brushing and flossing; recommend electric toothbrush, fluoride toothpaste + tooth mousse. Can't give varnish without consent for treatment. Diet history — ask frequency even if parent says diet is "very healthy"; developmental defects put her at higher risk.

Questions Patients Ask & Key Facts
  • "I am ready to pay, can you please treat her today?" / "Can you do a frenectomy for her?" / "What are we going to do today?" / "Does she have missing teeth?"
  • Ortho treatment is normally not covered under the Child Benefit Scheme (CBS) unless there's a craniofacial condition, cleft lip, multiple missing teeth, or another qualifying medical condition.
  • No wait period at the hospital for this referral pathway — seen on a priority basis (special government funding).
  • "Diagnosis we cannot give you, as it's confidential — let's check with the organisation."
  • References: congenital syphilis (early/late, vulnerable individuals); consent → foster care/kinship — caries risk assessment article; ADC guidance notes on intellectual disability.

RCT Patient Wants Extraction Instead — Mr/Mrs Argenti, 55PQ-9

Module 2, Cluster 1. Built on a sinusitis differential. Severe pain top-right; RCT-treated tooth in the area; OPG taken.

Patient & Scenario

Complaint: is the pain different from before? Presenting complaint: halitosis, pain. Smoking/past smoker; alcohol. Not given: hay fever ↔ no hay fever; chest infections (sometimes). Patient could be stubborn: "Remove my tooth, I don't want to answer anything."

Pain scenarios: pain different from before (odontogenic); no change in pain after RCT (hay fever); pain started after RCT and continues (odontogenic).

How to Start / Questions to Ask

"Mr Argenti, I know how frustrating it can be that despite undergoing treatment you are still having pain — let's explore together to figure out what could be causing this pain."

  1. Have you noticed any nasal congestion? How long has this been going on? Have you done anything about it?
  2. Smoking/history of smoking? If quit, why? Is this pain different from what you had earlier? Could you describe this pain?
  3. "Can I make that tooth numb to help you with the pain? It'll also help us differentiate if the pain is from the sinus or tooth." (selective anaesthesia — only partially reduces pain if the sinus is involved)
  4. "I am happy to remove this tooth for you but only if we are able to localize the tooth. If the anaesthesia only partially reduces the pain, then it could be coming from the structures around the tooth."
  5. Any other problems — halitosis/bad breath/congestion? Have you done anything for the pain? Any fever/unwell?

Diagnosis & Investigation

Differentials: odontogenic — vertical root fracture (VRF), failed RCT; non-odontogenic — sinusitis (localised/unilateral, pain on bending), respiratory infection/hay fever (RCT normally has a high success rate; extra canals/bacteria left behind can explain a failure).

Investigation: pain on bending/touching, cloudy X-ray (extensive/localised); extra-oral — swelling, fever, palpate jaw; intra-oral — examine all fillings/gum; cold test, palpation, percussion, mobility; probing (VRF), bite test, selective anaesthesia; X-ray PA + specialist CBCT. If fever is present, GP can prescribe antibiotics for sinusitis.

Management

Referral to an endodontist who will work with ENT to find the cause. Can't remove the tooth without a confirmed diagnosis.

Health promotion: address/check infection; causes of halitosis (alcohol cessation); regular dental review and checks; if hay fever isn't being treated, this becomes part of preventive advice.
