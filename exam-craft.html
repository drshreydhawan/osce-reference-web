Exam-Craft Tips

The specific, non-obvious moves that actually move the needle in the room.

Opening & structure

  • First minute is the golden minute — aim for ~20 sec you talking, 40 sec the patient talking
  • Start engaging and impressive — this sets the tone for the whole station
  • Ideas → Concerns → Expectations: "What are you worried about?" surfaces concerns → "What do you expect from me?" closes the loop
  • Have a structured approach but stay ready to adapt to the situation
  • Finish in ~9 minutes — save the last minute for summary, health promotion, and review
  • Last 90 seconds: recap today, prescription, brochures, preventive advice, review appointment

Language & tone

  • Avoid jargon and slang — plain English, scientific terms only as a follow-up
  • No abbreviations out loud ("mg", "mm") — say it in full
  • Acknowledge disclosures explicitly: "Thanks for sharing that" — encourages patients to keep sharing
  • Frequently check understanding — don't just list facts, ask if they follow
  • Paraphrase their question back before answering

Reasoning out loud

  • Explain why you're suggesting a test/scan/x-ray/treatment — logical reasoning, not just the answer
  • Talk about what you can do first, before talking about referral
  • Before referring, explain why you're referring and to which specialist
  • If something's shown on-screen (photo/x-ray), explicitly reference and explain it to the patient
  • If something's mentioned on-screen, confirm it with the patient rather than assuming it's true

Reading the room

  • Watch what the examiner is doing (coughing, avoiding eye contact) — can be a signal
  • When the patient checks their prompt sheet and asks something specific, treat it as important and answer it immediately, wherever you are in the flow
  • Ask permission before advising: "How does smoking fit into your life?" before jumping to cessation advice
  • Detach from what the examiner thinks of you — focus on the patient in front of you

Content & safety

  • Present the full range of treatment options with pros/cons of each — don't default to one
  • Ask their preference before presenting options, where appropriate
  • Check prerequisites before referring to a specialist (x-ray, OPG, basic history) — don't refer prematurely
  • Empower the patient — give "homework" (things to do before next visit)
  • Address every presenting complaint, not just the obvious one

Mindset

  • Perform as you normally would — don't try to "sound better" for the exam
  • You can't sound like everybody else — let your own voice come through
  • Humour is a legitimate defence against nerves, used sparingly
  • It's fine to be open about a gap in knowledge rather than bluff
  • Empathise and stay sensitive — this materially affects pass/fail
  • Avoid being overtly apologetic or paternalistic — treat them as a partner, not a problem to manage

Complaints, Angry Patients & Breaking Bad News

If a patient wants to complain

  1. Talk to your dentist directly
  2. Raise concerns with the practice manager
  3. Submit a complaint to AHPRA
  4. Submit a complaint to the Health Complaints Commissioner

Code of conduct when something's gone wrong: recognise what happened → act immediately to rectify → explain what happened and the short/long-term consequences → listen → acknowledge and support → comply with reporting requirements (advice from indemnity insurer) → review the adverse event and reduce recurrence risk → report to relevant authorities → ensure the patient knows how to make a formal complaint.

De-escalating an angry patient

Build rapport in the first minute. Learn to (politely) take control if they won't stop talking. When apologising for an adverse event:

  1. Acknowledge — "I'm sorry this happened"
  2. Give a factual explanation of what happened
  3. Give them space to relate their own experience
  4. Explain potential consequences of the event
  5. Explain the steps being taken to manage it and prevent recurrence

Breaking bad news

Give information in short chunks (20–30 seconds), then pause — let them process and respond before continuing. Don't rush to fill silence.

Consent Essentials

Every consent conversation should cover

  1. Nature of the disease/condition
  2. Nature of the investigation/test
  3. All treatment options (including doing nothing)
  4. Risks of each option
  5. Likely sequelae of each option
  6. Your preferred/recommended option — stating a clear recommendation is a major, commonly-missed part of informed consent
  7. Treatment time
  8. Cost of treatment
  9. Confirm the patient understands all of the above and has had the chance to ask questions

Implied consent covers a basic examination only — any extra tests need separate, explicit consent. Confidentiality should be reaffirmed where relevant (e.g. discussing a minor's care, third parties present).

Clinical Crib Notes

Fast-reference facts for topics that come up repeatedly.

Smoking

Smokers are ~3× more likely to lose all their teeth. Oral effects: halitosis, gum disease, dulled taste/smell, slow healing post-extraction, oral cancer, staining, decay. General effects: lung cancer, emphysema, bronchitis, heart disease.

Advice framing: ask permission first ("Can I give you more information?"), respect a "no," offer to revisit later. Assist via Quitline (behavioural support, they call the patient) and GP-prescribed medication + self-help material.

Pathophysiology: smoking causes keratinisation of the oral mucosa; on quitting, thinning can transiently increase ulceration risk (worth mentioning nicotine replacement therapy). Heavy smoking impairs healing via carbon monoxide, tissue hypoxia, and hydrogen cyanide — greater consumption/duration = greater significance as an MRONJ co-factor. Nicotine is addictive via a dopaminergic mechanism similar to cocaine; withdrawal symptoms appear as blood nicotine drops between cigarettes.

Reference: DHSV "Smoke-Free Smiles"; Smoking Cessation Guidelines for Australian General Practice.

TMD / Bruxism — 3-question screener

  1. Do you have pain in your temple, face, jaw, or jaw joints at least once a week?
  2. Do you have pain when opening your mouth or chewing, at least once a week?
  3. Does your jaw lock or become stuck?

Pain character — quick differential

  • Specific, localised pain → likely tooth
  • Non-specific pain → likely gums
  • Radiating, dull pain → likely muscle

Fluoride varnish — how to explain it

"It's a fluoride paint we apply to your teeth. It has 22,600 ppm fluoride — much higher than toothpaste — and it strengthens your enamel, making it more resistant to acid attack."

Gingival enlargement — drug causes

Calcium channel blockers (e.g. amlodipine, used for hypertension) are a classic cause of gingival enlargement. Also relevant for hypertensive patients: those on ACE inhibitors (perindopril, captopril) or diuretics — and note NSAIDs should be avoided in this group (renal risk).

Stress & periodontal health

Stress affects periodontal health via behaviour change (poorer oral hygiene, more clenching/grinding, increased smoking) and directly via nervous/endocrine/immune interactions — chronic stress and poor coping reduce salivary flow and suppress immunity. Elevated salivary cortisol/reactive oxygen species has been linked to ulcer onset.

Diabetes — perio relevance

Diabetes impairs the body's immune response, reduces gingival blood flow, and causes dry mouth — all of which accelerate periodontal disease progression. Diabetes medications work by moving sugar from the blood into cells. Type 1 diabetics tend to show more destruction around first molars, since these erupt first and have been exposed longest.
